● Colours in nature serve many purposes. Plants use green to capture light for food. Chlorophyll, the green pigment in
  leaves, absorbs red and blue light. It reflects green light, so most leaves look green to us. Flowers use bright
  colours to attract bees, butterflies and birds. These animals help the plant spread its pollen and seeds. Red, yellow
  and orange blossoms often signal a rich supply of nectar. Some flowers reflect ultraviolet light, which bees can see
  but people cannot. Animals also use colour to survive. A frog can be bright yellow to warn predators that its skin is
  toxic. A chameleon can change its skin colour to hide or to signal to other chameleons. Many moths have brown and grey
  wings that blend into tree bark. Blue colours in nature are rare. Most blue feathers, such as those of the blue jay,
  do not contain blue pigment. Instead, tiny structures in the feathers scatter light and make them look blue. The sky
  and the sea look blue for a similar reason. Light scatters off water molecules and air particles, and blue light
  scatters the most. Autumn leaves show yellow, orange and red. These colours were hidden by green chlorophyll during
  the summer. As the days get shorter, trees stop making chlorophyll, and the other pigments show. Colour in nature is
  not only beautiful. It is a useful tool for finding food, avoiding danger and finding a mate.
