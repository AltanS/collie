
  You wait again. Three minutes, maybe four. The waiting is part of the tea.

  Steam rises. You smell it first. Some teas smell like hay. Some smell like smoke, or flowers, or
  toast. Some smell like rain on warm stone.

  You lift the cup. It is hot. You take a small sip. The taste comes in layers. First there is a
  soft bitterness. Then a little sweetness follows. Last comes a dry feeling on the tongue. That dry
  feeling comes from tannin. Tannin is a plant compound. It is also in wine and in oak.

  People have done this for thousands of years. In China, monks drank tea to stay awake through long
  hours of thought. In Japan, people built a quiet ceremony around one bowl. In Britain, tea became
  a meal in the afternoon. In Morocco, hosts pour mint tea from high above the glass. In India,
  street vendors boil it with milk, sugar, and spice.

  The forms change. The core stays the same. Hot water meets a leaf, and someone stops to wait.

  Perhaps that is the real gift of tea. It does not rush you. It gives you a few minutes with
  nothing to do. You hold something warm. You breathe in. You let the day slow down.

  Then the cup is empty. You can fill it again.
