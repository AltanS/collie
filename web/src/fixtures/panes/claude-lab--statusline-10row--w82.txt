  retreated into a niche and thrived there. Unix systems, servers, and developer
  workflows relied on shells, and the rise of the internet meant that
  administrators connected to remote machines through SSH, a text channel. The
  terminal emulator became a window inside the GUI, and the pairing proved
  remarkably durable.

  [0m[1mThe Modern Revival[0m

  Since roughly 2010, terminal interfaces have enjoyed a renaissance. Several
  forces contributed. Developers spend much of their day in version control,
  containers, and cloud tooling, all command-driven. Terminal multiplexers like
  tmux and editors like Vim and Neovim built loyal followings. Modern terminals
  gained truecolor support, Unicode, images, hyperlinks, and GPU rendering, as
  with Kitty, Alacritty, WezTerm, and iTerm2.

  New toolkits reflect this energy: Rust's ratatui, Go's Bubble Tea, Python's
  Textual, and JavaScript's Ink. Applications such as lazygit, k9s, btop, and htop
  offer rich, keyboard-driven dashboards that start instantly and work over SSH.
  More recently, AI coding assistants have chosen the terminal as their home, a
  sign that the medium still fits workflows that value speed, scriptability, and
  composition.

  [0m[1mConclusion[0m

  The history of the terminal user interface is a story of constraints turning
  into virtues. Teletypes gave us the command line; glass terminals gave us cursor
  addressing; the VT100 gave us a shared standard; curses gave us portability;
  and the PC gave us the text-mode application. Today's terminal tools inherit all
  of it. Beneath their polish, they still send escape sequences to a grid of
  character cells, much as a 1978 VT100 did, which suggests that the idea has
  staying power.
