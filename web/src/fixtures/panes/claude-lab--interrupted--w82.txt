[0m[38;2;255;255;255m●[0m [0m[1m[3m[4mThe History of Terminal User Interfaces[0m

  The terminal user interface, or TUI, is among the longest-lived ideas in
  computing. It has been declared obsolete many times and keeps returning. Its
  history shows how people have tried to talk to machines using little more than a
  grid of characters.

  [0m[1mThe Teletype Era[0m

  Before screens, there were printers. In the 1960s, interactive computing usually
  meant a teletypewriter such as the ASR-33. A user typed a command, the machine
  clicked and clattered, and the reply was printed on paper. The interface was a
  scrolling transcript. Nothing could be erased, redrawn, or repositioned, so
  interaction was linear. Many conventions we still use come from this period. The
  line-oriented shell, the idea of "standard input and output," and even the name
  "tty" all descend from these devices.

  [0m[1mGlass Teletypes and Addressable Cursors[0m

  The arrival of cathode-ray tube terminals in the 1970s changed what was
  possible. Early "glass teletypes" merely imitated paper, but terminals such as
  the DEC VT52 (1975) and later the VT100 (1978) added cursor addressing. A
  program could now place text anywhere on the screen by sending special character
  sequences. The VT100 popularized what became ANSI escape codes. These sequences
  move the cursor, clear regions, and set attributes such as bold, underline, and
  reverse video. The ANSI X3.64 standard codified them, and today's terminal
  emulators still honor them.

  This made full-screen programs possible. Bill Joy's [0m[38;2;177;185;249mvi [0m(1976) was written for
  terminals like these, and [0m[38;2;177;185;249memacs [0mfollowed in various forms. Their modal editing
  and keybindings reflect an environment with limited bandwidth and no mouse.
